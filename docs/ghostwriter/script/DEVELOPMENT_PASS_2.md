**Why Mara's two scenes became one.** The first draft had her arrive, examine the arm and the page, take two pages away, and then explain what she found on a video call from her lab with a pencil-hardness grid (4B, HB, 2H). The second scene said again what the first had shown. Now she drives up because June phoned her after the near-drowning ("Five hours. She called me at midnight," p. 33), reads the arm, the paper and the back of the page, and lays two pages side by side (p. 34): page one, which Theo has never copied from, has dark, deep pencil on its back; the sheet he did copy from is a faint gray. "Every line that comes up on you, the pencil on my pages gets lighter. You're writing over someone." The audience sees the trade across a kitchen table. Her phone photograph of the back comes out blank paper.

**Why I stopped at three pages and not five.** What is left in Act Two is either a plant paid off in Act Three (the whistle, the middle name, the 3:22 time card, the library's Marit line, the photograph), a set piece the review asked me to protect, or a short visual beat that changes the world (the diner morning, the pond). Getting to five pages would mean thinning the bathroom or cutting the diner morning or the pond; I recommend against that until you have heard the pages read aloud. The three scenes I would listen to first are Mara, Walter and the mirror (pp. 32–42): three two-person scenes in a row, and the part of the middle most likely to feel long.

## 6. One line in the Verso

When Theodore tells Theo what the Study holds, he now begins: "Do you know what this room is? The back of the page, where everything it didn't say goes. Everything you dropped on the way here." (p. 69). It answers Walter's definition (below), so the audience hears the title's meaning before the Verso opens and again as they stand in it.

When Theodore holds out the pen, Theo now asks "And me?" and Theodore answers "You'll be very happy." (p. 72). The cost to Theo himself is said aloud, and the answer is the one promise in the film that is a lie. This is the only other change to Act Three.

## 7. June's decisions

Before this pass June acted (she read the page, offered the memory theory, called her mother, saved Theo's life), but at the one moment she could choose she asked permission ("Can I stay?") and Mara set the terms. She now makes two choices, and both are a twelve-year-old's.

1. **She stays, and sets the terms** (p. 35). Mara: "I came to take you home." June: "I'm staying. He can't do this by himself. I'll call you at nine. Every night." She negotiates a ritual, not a plan, and where the first draft had her ask, she now tells. It costs her the way out, and it is the reason the sticky note on the refrigerator (CALL MOM 9) and the palms of her hands (Act Three, p. 68) exist.
2. **She phones Ellie, and her father does not stop her** (p. 57). After the Turnover, June: "The nurse said she was there. At the diner." Theo: "It's the middle of the night." June: "She works nights." She taps the screen. This is the call that brings Nell to the back door at 3:58 (p. 58: "Your daughter called the station. I walked from the highway"), so the end of Act Two is something June caused, not something that happened to her.

Neither decision solves anything, and each brings an adult into the house. That is how a child's agency works in this story.

## 8. The sixth dimension and the title

**The problem.** The title was never explained in the film. The bible has a full ladder of dimensions; the script said nothing about it.

**What the film says now** (p. 38), in Walter's study, with his fingers: "A line, one dimension. A page, two. A book, three. The time it takes to read it, four. Every way it might have gone, five. The sixth is the back of the page, where everything it didn't say goes." Theo: "That's a metaphor." Walter: "It's a filing system. Whoever's holding yours has been pressing hard." The wryness keeps it out of lecture. Theodore repeats it in the Study (p. 69).

**The title is unchanged.** It does work now: the first act introduces a ghostwriter, the second names the dimension, the third opens it. Before you change it, three things from the bible (section 0.8) still hold. *The Ghost Writer* (Polanski, 2010) exists, so a title search is needed either way. The long title can read as pulp to a cold reader. And **VERSO** is the bible's choice for release.

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| THE GHOSTWRITER FROM THE 6TH DIMENSION (current) | Distinctive; "ghostwriter" does real work; now explained in the film | Pulp tone to a cold reader; long |
| VERSO | One word; exact; the film's structure (it turns over) | The word is never spoken in the script. If you choose it, add one word to Walter's line: "The sixth is the back of the page. The verso." |
| THE MARGIN | Theo's boyhood notebook, and the margin where Tomas wrote | Quiet; says nothing about horror |
| CHIEF READER | The signature in the notebook, and the film's emotional center | Gives nothing away, and signals no genre |
| TURN IT OVER | The bible's tagline; an instruction | Reads as a tagline, not a name |

My recommendation has not changed: keep the long title for development and festival submission, and decide on VERSO when a distributor is in the room.
